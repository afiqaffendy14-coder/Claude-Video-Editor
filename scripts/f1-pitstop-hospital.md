### How it holds attention
- 0–3 s: an odd pairing (children's heart surgeon + Ferrari) as a question the viewer can't answer.
- Every 5–10 s a turn re-raises the stakes: "But the danger isn't over." / "Then..." / "So they called Ferrari." / "The result?"
- The pit stop is described in the same rhythm as the handover (short counts: "Twenty mechanics. One car. A few seconds.")
  so the viewer makes the connection a beat before it's said.
- Payoff numbers land after "The result?", then an emotional line, then the loop.

## Facts and sources

- Doctors: heart surgeon Martin Elliott and intensive care head Allan Goldman, Great Ormond Street Hospital (GOSH), London.
- They visited Ferrari in Maranello; Ferrari's team analysed the hospital's handovers.
- The anaesthetist became the single leader of the handover, modelled on the lollipop man; the protocol also drew on aviation.
  Principles: leadership, task allocation, rhythm, standardised process, checklists, communication.
- Results (WSJ / Catchpole et al., *Paediatric Anaesthesia* 2007, about 50 handovers): technical errors per handover
  fell 42% (5.42 to 3.15), information omissions fell 49%.
- Caveat: the study did not measure deaths, so the script says "safer", never "saved lives".
- Not verified, so left out: the story that they first saw the pit stop on TV after a long operation.
