# Story formats for F1 Shorts

A reference for choosing a story shape before writing a script. It's built from a research pass on 2026-11-08 that
used creator interviews, the leaked MrBeast production guide, screenwriting rules and the University of Vermont
story-arc study. It summarises what creators *say* they do. It isn't a frame-by-frame study of their videos.

## The owner's format: the "rollercoaster" (a chain of reversals)

Hook → rising action → conflict → comeback → climax → conflict → comeback → rising action → end

There's no single official name. It combines three known ideas:
- **Vonnegut's "Cinderella" arc** (rise → fall → rise), repeated. Each conflict → comeback pair is a
  **"Man in a Hole"** (fall → rise).
- **Sanderson's try-fail cycles** ("yes, but" / "no, and"): each attempt succeeds with a catch, or fails and makes
  things worse.
- **South Park's "but / therefore" rule**: every beat links to the last with *but* (a setback) or *therefore* (a
  consequence), never *and then*.

**Shorts rules for this format:**
1. Two full fall-and-rise cycles is the most 60 s can hold. Each beat gets about 5–8 s.
2. Each fall must be bigger than the one before. The second conflict has to hurt more than the first.
3. The biggest beat comes last. After the final comeback, deliver the payoff and cut (Hoyos: end right after the
   promise is kept). Don't add a falling action or a wrap-up.
4. Test every join: if "and then" fits between two beats, the story is sagging.

## Other shapes

| Shape | Flow | Best for | F1 example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Man in a Hole | fine → disaster → recovery | survivals and comebacks | Kubica, Montreal 2007 → 2008 |
| Cinderella | rise → fall → bigger rise | underdogs and dreams | Hamilton's autograph book |
| Icarus | rise → fall | cautionary tales, hubris | a team that won everything, then vanished |
| Oedipus / tragedy | fall → rise → final fall | heartbreak and cruel twists | Massa, champion for 30 seconds |
| In medias res | start at the peak moment → rewind → catch up → payoff | anything with one big visual moment | open on the last-corner pass, then "ten years earlier…" |
| Promise → payoff | the hook promises something → build → deliver → cut | short, single-twist stories (30–40 s) | "this driver couldn't legally drive to his own race" |
| Mystery box | question → clues → wrong answer → real answer | bizarre facts | three drivers with the exact same lap time |
| Then vs now | same place or person, two moments | callbacks and full-circle stories | the same wall, one year later |
| Escalating list | example 1 < 2 < 3, with the wildest last | streaks and records | the driver hit by his own safety car twice |

## What top creators say they do

- **MrBeast (leaked "How to Succeed in MrBeast Production" guide, 2024):** the opening is where most viewers leave,
  so it must match the thumbnail's promise straight away. Keep up a constant progression, so stakes rise and
  nothing stands still.
- **Jenny Hoyos (Shorts specialist):** write the hook and the last line first, then fill in the middle. End right
  after the hook's promise is delivered. Every second counts, trimming dead seconds raises retention, and she uses
  "but/therefore" in place of "and then".
- **Mark Rober:** failure creates tension, so show the attempt that doesn't work. A known solution with a flaw
  motivates the next reveal. Tease the climax early, then build to it.
- **Kallaway (short-form hooks):** a three-part hook made of a context lean-in, a scroll-stopping interjection and a
  contrarian snapback. He alternates context and conflict all the way through.
- **Trey Parker and Matt Stone (NYU talk):** beats joined by "and then" are boring. Join them with "but" or
  "therefore".
- **Brandon Sanderson:** use "yes, but" and "no, and" to escalate. A warning from the same school of thought: if
  every beat adds a complication, the story collapses, so keep it to one thread.

## Checklist before writing
1. Which shape fits the true story? Never bend facts to fit a shape.
2. Write the hook line and the last line first.
3. List the beats and join them with BUT or THEREFORE.
4. Each fall must be bigger than the last, and the biggest beat goes last.
5. Cut after the payoff.
